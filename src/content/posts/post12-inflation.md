As the U.S. Central Bank and Bank of England seem certain to raise interest rates by 1/4 - 1/2 pt in December, Bruce C. Greenwald and Josepth E. Stiglitz offer a compelling model that we may not be able to rely on tightening monetary policy leading to a real cooling in prices as wielded by the Fed throughout the 1980s, 90s, and 2000s.

Going back to Volcker's turn atop the Fed, as long as the FOMC rates came out consistent and confident, growth and inflation seemed well swung by monetary policy. Higher interest rates and tighter policy when the economy was burning a little hot, lower interest rates and easing if the economy needed a little boost.

But, Greenwald and Stiglitz in *[Monetary Policy in a Phelps-Winter Economy](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w36008/w36008.pdf)* argue that as the economy developers further into a service-led economy, complete with customer customization (massive amounts of data leading to personalized products) and costs moving primarily into distribution and sales, prices may in fact *increase* along with interest rates.

The logic being that these types of firms, with a strong capacity to hold on to today's consumer and buck elasticity (at least the in the short term) would view rising interest rates as a major cost on their *future* market share and do everything they can to capitalize (profit) on their *current* customers leading to rising prices today.

They cite evidence going back to 2012, separating the response from the Goods and Services sectors in response to rising and lowering interest rates. Given the numbers line up, it paints a bleak picture for the Fed's ability to rein in overall prices specifically. They also note that the Goods sector has seen a proliferation of Services type activity throughout its supply chain (marketing and sales taking on a bigger portion of costs) so it's not even clear if higher interest rates could rein in even the portion of the economy that produces Goods.

Higher interest rates will lead to slower firm and stock growth, reduced loan opportunities that are already hitting house buying opportunities hard and may eventually start hitting wage and employment opportunities. But this has always come with the tradeoff that your bills would cool down and lead to a subsequent growth recovery where everyone benefits.
