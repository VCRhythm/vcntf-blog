## 

After World War II, Japan kept the yen fixed at **360 yen per dollar**. This is a commitment by the government to trade dollars for yen at 360 each and every day no matter what happens in the market. That changed the foreign price of Japanese goods relative to equivalent goods abroad.

Suppose a Japanese manufacturer produces something for **36,000 yen**. At 360 yen/dollar, the equivalent foreign price is $100.

* **Without a fixed rate**: if the yen suddenly strengthened to 180 yen/dollar (as the Japanese economy recovered after the war), the same 36,000 yen cost would be $200

* **With a fixed rate**: a currency can't just suddenly strengthen by drifting. The foreign buyer pays dollars, which the exporter converts back into yen at the official rate. The buyer knows they will pay $100

So Japanese goods remained priced below what the market would have charged even with a growing economy.

## The Cost

Japan’s post-war 360-yen rate worked like a subsidy, not in the sense of the government directly compensating exporters, but as a *cross-subsidy* that helped one group by making prices more expensive for another.

For an exporter, the policy did three things:

1. **It set the conversion rate.** An exporter could earn dollars abroad and bring home a predictable number of yen

2. **It supported a price advantage.** The exporter could sell abroad at a lower dollar price while still covering domestic yen costs

3. **It made foreign markets more reachable.** A low dollar price helped Japanese producers compete against firms whose costs were not being discounted through the exchange-rate system

But look at the other side of the equation. If the yen is kept weak, *imports* become expensive in yen terms. Take a $100 imported input:

- At **360 yen/dollar**, it costs a Japanese firm **36,000 yen**

- At **180 yen/dollar**, the same input would cost **18,000 yen**

Fuel, raw materials, food, and imported components all became more expensive than they would have been under a stronger yen.

That means the policy had a distributional structure:

**Winners**

* Exporters, because their goods looked cheaper abroad

* Domestic producers competing against imports, because imported goods looked more expensive

* Firms and policymakers focused on rebuilding industrial capacity and earning foreign exchange

**Losers**

* Households, because imported goods and materials cost more yen, and domestic goods can be sold at higher prices

* Import-dependent firms, because their input costs rose

The important point is not simply that Japan promoted exports. The cost of supporting the export sector had to be paid by anyone who relied on imported goods

This story gets even more interesting as Japan's economy grows to the point where the U.S. eventually says **enough**.

## The Nixon story

*Outline only — the sections and the beat each one carries. Prose to come.*

- **The setup.** By the late 1960s the subsidy had done its job. Japanese goods weren't only cheap anymore; they were getting better, the U.S. trade gap was widening, and gold was leaving the Treasury at a pace Washington wouldn't live with.

- **The break.** On August 15, 1971, Nixon suspends the dollar's convertibility into gold and adds a temporary 10 percent import surcharge. The dollar is the unit every fixed rate stands on. Now the unit itself is unanchored, and the yen's 360 is a rate Japan is defending inside a system the United States just left.

- **The squeeze.** At the Smithsonian talks in December 1971, Japan is pressed into re-pegging at 308 — a revaluation of about 17 percent, coming from the country that had been paying the bill. The surcharge comes off. The market keeps pushing, and by February 1973 the yen floats.

- **Who gains, who loses.** In the U.S., relief for firms competing against Japanese imports, higher prices for the shoppers who buy them. In Japan, exporters face 36,000-yen costs selling in dollars 17 percent weaker — the cross-subsidy reverses direction overnight.

- **The productivity test.** A revaluation only ruins an industry that needed the weak rate to hide what it couldn't do. Japan's exporters kept improving at 308 — the cheap price got customers in the door, and the improving product kept them.

- **The fragile assumption.** This subsidy was never Japan's to keep. It lasted exactly as long as the payers were willing to pay, and in 1971 they stopped.

- **The data gap.** How much of the 1960s export boom was the peg, and how much was productivity? 1971 is as close to an experiment as we get: the yen revalued about 17 percent in months, and exports kept growing anyway.

- **One idea to carry away.** A subsidy ends when the side paying it decides it costs too much. The series question still applies: who gains, who loses, and through what channel — and now, who is still willing to pick up the bill?
