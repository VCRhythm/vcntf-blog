## The Nixon story

*Outline only — the sections and the beat each one carries. Prose to come.*

- **The setup.** By the late 1960s the subsidy had done its job. Japanese goods weren't only cheap anymore; they were getting better, the U.S. trade gap was widening, and gold was leaving the Treasury at a pace Washington wouldn't live with.

- **The break.** On August 15, 1971, Nixon suspends the dollar's convertibility into gold and adds a temporary 10 percent import surcharge. The dollar is the unit every fixed rate stands on. Now the unit itself is unanchored, and the yen's 360 is a rate Japan is defending inside a system the United States just left.

- **The squeeze.** At the Smithsonian talks in December 1971, Japan is pressed into re-pegging at 308 — a revaluation of about 17 percent, coming from the country that had been paying the bill. The surcharge comes off. The market keeps pushing, and by February 1973 the yen floats.

- **Who gains, who loses.** In the U.S., relief for firms competing against Japanese imports, higher prices for the shoppers who buy them. In Japan, exporters face 36,000-yen costs selling in dollars 17 percent weaker — the cross-subsidy reverses direction overnight.

- **The productivity test.** A revaluation only ruins an industry that needed the weak rate to hide what it couldn't do. Japan's exporters kept improving at 308 — the cheap price got customers in the door, and the improving product kept them.

- **The fragile assumption.** This subsidy was never Japan's to keep. It lasted exactly as long as the payers were willing to pay, and in 1971 they stopped.

- **The data gap.** How much of the 1960s export boom was the peg, and how much was productivity? 1971 is as close to an experiment as we get: the yen revalued about 17 percent in months, and exports kept growing anyway.

- **One idea to carry away.** A subsidy ends when the side paying it decides it costs too much. The series question still applies: who gains, who loses, and through what channel — and now, who is still willing to pick up the bill?
