**A note on framing**: this is a thought experiment. China's Treasury holdings have been falling, but nothing in the data says it is preparing a break with the dollar. The point is to trace the chain of consequences if it did.

## Why inflation is the trigger
![[Pasted image 20261110122240.png]]
Mainland China held **$590.7 billion** of U.S. Treasuries in August 2026, the third-largest foreign holder behind Japan ($1.07 trillion) and the UK ($1.01 trillion). A year earlier it held $699.7 billion, so holdings are down about **16%**, and $103.5 billion of that came off since February. Those holdings exist because the dollar is liquid, deep, and stable in *real* terms.

One caveat on the number: the Treasury table shows value at the end of each month, so it can't separate actual selling from price declines as yields rose. It also undercounts anything China holds through custodians in places like Belgium.

Inflation attacks the last part. A Treasury paying 4% while prices rise 6% is a guaranteed loss of purchasing power for the holder. If that gap persisted, the largest foreign holders would have a reason to ask whether the dollar is still the right place to park a trade surplus.

We aren't at 6%, but the direction matters. October CPI rose **0.4%** for the month, a tenth above the 0.3% consensus, and is up **3.72%** from a year ago. Annualized, a single month at that pace is nearly 5%, well above the year-over-year rate.

## What "leaving the dollar bloc" would actually mean

It isn't one switch. It's three separate decisions:

1. **Reserves**: stop buying Treasuries, and eventually sell. Shift toward gold, other currencies, hard assets
2. **Currency management**: stop steering the yuan against the dollar and let it float or track a basket
3. **Trade settlement**: price and settle more trade in yuan, through systems like CIPS instead of dollar-based channels

Each has a different cost, and China can do them at very different speeds.

## Consequences for the U.S.

* **Higher borrowing costs.** The Treasury market relies on foreign demand. Less of it means higher yields, which feeds directly into the story from my last post: the government is already competing with AI borrowers for the same pool of savings
* **A weaker dollar.** Fewer buyers means a lower price, so imports cost more in dollar terms
* **Inflation feeding on itself.** The trigger was inflation, and the response (a weaker dollar) *raises* import prices. The Fed would face a choice between tolerating more inflation and raising rates into a market that is already stressed

## Consequences for China

This is where the exit gets difficult, and it connects to the 360-yen story from earlier in this series.

* **A stronger yuan is a tax on exporters.** China's export machine benefited for decades from a managed currency. Letting it appreciate is the reverse of Japan's fixed rate: exporters lose their price advantage, importers and households gain purchasing power
* **Self-inflicted losses.** Every dollar sold into the market pushes down the value of the dollars China still holds. The largest holder has the most to lose from a disorderly sell-off
* **No ready substitute.** The yuan isn't freely convertible and China maintains capital controls. A reserve currency requires deep, open markets where holders can enter and leave. Beijing would have to give up some control to get one

## Everyone else

Third countries would be forced to pick: hold more gold, diversify into euros and other currencies, or keep hoarding dollars. Commodity pricing (oil especially) would be the real test, since it is where dollar demand is most entrenched.

The closest historical parallel is not a country leaving, but the **1985 Plaza Accord**, where the U.S. pressured Japan and others into a stronger currency. Japan's appreciation was then followed by asset bubbles and decades of slow growth. A forced yuan appreciation would carry the same risk.

## What would have to be true

For this scenario to be real rather than hypothetical:

- U.S. inflation would need to stay well above target *and* look unanchored, not just a temporary spike
- China would need to decide that the domestic costs of exiting (weaker exports, capital-control loosening) were smaller than the cost of holding depreciating assets
- Alternatives would need to be deep enough to absorb trillions of dollars of reserves

The IMF's reserve data shows how far the alternatives are from that. In the second quarter of 2026 the dollar was **56.70%** of the world's $13.22 trillion in reserves (about $7.5 trillion), down from 57.18% a quarter earlier. The euro rose to 20.60%, mostly from active purchasing. The renminbi was **2.11%**, up from 1.98%.

So the drift away from the dollar is real but small, and the yuan is nowhere near big enough to take the dollar's place. That third condition is the binding one, and it's why the more likely outcome is a slow drift (a gradually smaller dollar share of reserves) rather than a clean break.
