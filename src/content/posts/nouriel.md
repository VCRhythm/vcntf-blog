On September 7, 2006, Nouriel Roubini gave a talk at the International Monetary Fund. The United States, he told the room, was heading for a housing bust. Households had spent their savings and were living on rising debt, house prices had climbed far faster than incomes, and the country was borrowing from the rest of the world to cover the gap. When the bubble broke, he said, it would destroy household wealth, choke off spending, and leave a weakened banking system facing a recession severe enough to shake the global financial system.

The room did not agree. A fellow panellist from the Economic Cycle Research Institute was not persuaded, and the general view was that this New York University professor was being needlessly dark. The IMF's own magazine covered the talk under the title "Meet Dr. Doom" and noted that Roubini had cheerfully adopted the name himself. The nickname stuck.

The bust arrived on something close to the schedule he had described. Subprime mortgage failures took down Bear Stearns in March 2008 and Lehman Brothers that September, and the recession he had outlined began. Since then, "Dr. Doom" has been shorthand for the economist who saw 2008 coming.

The habit behind the call was reading balance sheets. Roubini watched household savings (near zero), household debt (rising fast), and the US current-account deficit (large), then asked what happens when buyers who depend on borrowed money run out of credit. His books carry the same idea. Crisis Economics, written with Stephen Mihm after 2008, argues that financial crises are recurring features of market economies, more predictable in kind than in timing. Megathreats (2022) widened the list of dangers: runaway debt, deglobalisation, climate change, AI displacing work.

Teach the misses with the hit. Roubini warned repeatedly of a double-dip recession after the 2009 recovery had begun; growth held. He predicted for years that the euro was about to break apart; the euro survived. In 2020 he argued the pandemic recession would be worse than the Great Depression; it turned out to be the shortest on record, and the recovery brought the highest inflation in four decades. A forecaster who warns every year will eventually collect a hit, and 2008 is remembered while the false alarms fade. Judged on the full record, he is a careful reader of balance sheets who leans dark — neither an oracle nor a crank.

His current work is built differently from the 2006 call. In November 2025 he set out three paths for the US economy in 2026: tariffs and policy chaos tipping into stagflation; a hard landing if AI investment disappoints; or a soft landing in which AI investment is strong enough to carry growth while tariffs drag it down. The detail that made news is that he put the highest odds on the most positive path and argued that AI's productivity gains could outweigh the combined drag of tariffs, fiscal risk, and geopolitics by a four-to-one ratio. The man nicknamed Doom now ranks a soft landing as the likeliest outcome.

Three things to take from his observations. Watch balance sheets, because leverage decides how much damage a shock does. Judge forecasters on their full record, 2008 and the false alarms included. And prefer scenarios to point predictions: his current work names several paths and what would move the economy between them, which is more usable than a single number. The lesson of the observations is less about doom than about reading the accounts while everyone else watches the mood.
