Retail sales rose 0.2% in October, down from a revised 0.8% gain in September, according to the Commerce Department’s report released Tuesday.

![[Pasted image 20261117110804.png]]
*Retail sales, millions of nominal dollars, December 2023 to October 2026. The level keeps rising; adjusted for prices, October spending fell.*

September’s revised 0.8% now looks like the outlier, and October’s 0.2% is weaker than it looks.

Retail sales are nominal — they count dollars spent, not goods bought. CPI came in at 0.4% for the month, so a 0.2% spending gain means *real* spending fell. Households bought less and paid more for it. That is the demand side softening with prices still climbing.

A soft goods print calms the inflation worry in one narrow place only, for three reasons:

* Retail sales cover goods sold at stores and miss most *services*, which is the bulk of consumption. This doesn’t settle overall demand.

* The supply side hasn’t loosened. ISM shows supplier deliveries slowed for 11 straight months through October, manufacturers hoarding materials, and a prices index at 75.2. 

The pressure behind this series’ rate story was never mainly shoppers. The price of money has been driven up throughout 2026 by AI companies borrowing for land, compute, and electricity while  governments keep widening deficits partly to cover growing energy costs. This buildout may be cooling. AI-linked share prices have fallen and Nvidia may reduce the amount of money that it puts into businesses that buy and supply it. If that spending does slow, the competition for savings eases, and the rate pressure from capital demand softens along with it.

## Who pays when spending loses to inflation

When prices outrun spending, the squeeze lands first on households that are alraedy rationing.

## What it means for December

The Fed held at 3.75% to 4% in October over two hawkish dissents, and Waller has said he would back a quarter-point hike in December. A softer consumer weakens the demand half of that case, but the Fed’s price evidence keeps arriving from the supply side: PPI later this week, and November CPI next month. A second soft month would confirm real demand has turned; a bounce back toward September’s pace would suggest October was a payback month instead.
