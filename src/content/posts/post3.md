I get sick of seeing a rise and fall explained using the same narrative. I get sick of seeing history waived off. I feel sick.

**This week**: ISM releases the manufacturing index; Apple, AMD, and Palantir release earnings; Labor department releasing September job openings and October jobs report; Bank of England decides on UK interest rates.

Ultimately, the price is the price is the price, but why is it the price? So many headlines wave away a stock movement as some part of the broader sector move, but at the same time don't explain why STOCK A *didn't* follow the broader sector (see AAPL last week)

## Scoreboards and pressure maps

Economic reporting loves scoreboards: GDP, inflation, unemployment, the S&P 500, consumer confidence because they're so damn easy. In games where numbers are loud and easy to measure, basic analysts put weekly player counts up on a chart and beam as they shopped it around to design departments. These stats carry information, but are meaningless if not taken a step deeper. In games, it's 2 steps deeper that matter: 1. the number of weekly players playing X days a week, then 2. the number of weekly players *transitioning* to play more or fewer days per week.

Economic stats should be read the same way. What's under the rock?

GDP can rise because productive capacity expanded, because credit expanded, because the government borrowed, because inventories changed, because a natural disaster forced rebuilding, or because a firm shipped production overseas. Inflation can rise from an energy shock, from money demand collapsing, from tariffs, from supply bottlenecks, from profit margins, or from demand outrunning supply. High interest rates can be the result of a central bank trying to curb inflation risk or a boom in capital-seeking technologies (hello 2026)!

And these stats are reported in a way that rarely references recent events let alone a historical compendium of how many times the same or similar event(s) happened before. It should be a bigger deal that the Fed changed the way they measure whether inflation was 2% in 2020. The switch to an *average* inflation goal has been met with shrugged acceptance and hope that inflation will magically reset and we can all just ignore the period of time (now!) when we pretended a number larger than 2% was *actually* 2% because we wanted it to be.

## Three levels of reasoning

The distinction I keep returning to comes from a causal way of thinking about evidence. It can be simplified into three questions.

### 1. What moves with what?

This is the ordinary level of public commentary. Two things happen together: the price of rent rises and construction also rises; unemployment falls and consumption rises; bond yields rise and stock valuations fall.

So an observation can say, "these things co-occurred." It cannot say, "this caused that."

### 2. What might happen if we change something?

This is the kind of reasoning that's taught in Economics grad programs and trains economists into thinking they don't just have an idea about what might happen if we change something, but hat they *know* what will happen if we change something. This is how you get so-called "libertarian" economists with a wry smile stating straight faced that inequality is good, that doing anything to try and reduce inequality will actually lead to *more* inequality, that economic policies that hurt billionaires will ultimately hurt the rest of society.

Econometrics is cool, and I love seeing an intuitive model of a dynamic system, but to treat these as anything other than possibilities in a post-Black Swan era is absolutely comical. We can ask about the world that did not happen, but since this did not happen, we just can't ever know it anywhere close to certainty. If unemployment fell after a policy, would it also have fallen without the policy? If yields rose after a buyback announcement, what would they have done in the absence of that announcement?

We've seen enough huge investment firms fail *this year* when yields rose as fast as they ever have despite being filled with big brained London boys at this point.

### 3. When something changes, what else changed and what caused that?

Any change, whether introduced by an intervention or not, never ever changes just one thing. And any observable change will create a chain reaction of changes. This is the one portion of macroeconomics that's useful to know. A tax cut may raise disposable income, but it may also push up debt-service expectations, alter inflation forecasts, shift currency markets, change investment plans or increase the rate of savings. A rate cut may reduce mortgage costs, but only if banks pass it through, borrowers qualify, housing supply responds, and households are not too worried to borrow. A war may end, signaling lower oil costs, but gas stations decide they like the price hikes and keep it for themselves (it's rough out there!)

## The price is all that matters

Mechanisms carry assumptions. Once you see the assumptions, you can ask where the story is fragile.

Take one common causal chain:

> The Central Bank reduced overnight rates, which now reduces the cost of borrowing. Cheaper borrowing increases housing demand. More demand raises housing prices. Higher housing wealth makes owners feel more secure and spend more.

But, now you're assuming that:

- Banks pass the policy rate cut through to mortgage rates
- The economy is healthy enough (which may not be true yet if interest rates are being cut to stimulate a flagging growth rate) that Borrowers can qualify and are willing to take on debt
- Housing Supply is not constrained and can expand enough to absorb the demand
- Owners actually spend more when their home value rises, rather than saving
- House prices remain liquid enough to be treated as wealth in the minds of households.

Break any of those, and the intended effect doesn't happen.

## What this series will do

I plan to return to a few questions again and again as I too wrestle with what's going on and what's next

1. **The mechanism.** What is changing?
2. **The distribution.** What's under that change and who gains and who loses?
3. **The data gap.** What are we treating as more "certain" that is really just a guess or model?
4. **The fragile assumption.** What conditions, if violated, change the conclusion?

That is the checklist I want to use for currency policy, interest rates, the AI capital buildout, housing, taxes, fiscal risk, market liquidity, labor income, and game economies. Different topics, same instinct: follow the pressure through the system and guess how this will play out in the year ahead?
